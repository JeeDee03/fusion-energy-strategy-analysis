## 3. Maintenance and Availability — Revised Analysis

The Stellaris conceptual engineering study describes a major remote-maintenance strategy involving approximately four full-power years of operation before replacement of major in-vessel components.

The study identifies a five-month target for major component replacement and an early seven-month estimate for the overall replacement campaign.

**Source:** S3_006 — Lion et al. (2025), Section 2.11, *Remote maintenance*.

The original Stage 3.3 model considered a four-to-six-year calendar cycle with a seven-month major outage. This produces a major-outage availability factor of approximately 85.4–90.3%, assuming the maintenance duration is included in the calendar cycle.

An alternative operating-cycle interpretation assumes four continuous years of operation followed by a seven-month maintenance outage:

\[
A_{\mathrm{major}}=\frac{48}{48+7}=87.27\%
\]

If the five-month maintenance target is achieved:

\[
A_{\mathrm{major}}=\frac{48}{48+5}=90.57\%
\]

These scenarios are not interchangeable. The calendar-cycle model defines the interval between outage starts, whereas the operating-cycle model defines operating time before an additional outage.

Furthermore, four full-power years represent accumulated full-power-equivalent operation and should not automatically be interpreted as four uninterrupted calendar years. The operating-cycle calculation therefore assumes continuous full-power operation for illustration.

**Research interpretation:** The results demonstrate the sensitivity of electricity production to scheduled major maintenance assumptions. They do not establish actual commercial plant availability or capacity factor. Minor maintenance, unplanned outages, commissioning, power variation, and other production losses remain excluded.

The Stage 4 economic model must retain these distinctions and represent further operational uncertainties explicitly.
